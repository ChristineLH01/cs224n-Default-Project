Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
When every private pauper dwells within his dwelling?
Why lov'st thou that which thou shouldst stray,
So far from home, thy manners so strange,
Stifling of the love thou dost impart?
Grant me the pardon, if I offend thee:
My love is strengthened by thy acquaintance,
And if that acquaintance turns against me,
My ruin is at its root.
  Wilt thou not pardon that I do defy?
  By self-example, thus, I learn to despise.

My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
And healing all rancors as if by honey.
My love is as the well that water doth pour,
Dispersing its contents all over the place,
Serving the sick and wounded alike.
It is the salve which wounds the heart,
Sanctioning the trespass of heart's desire,
Bathing in love's sweet cure,
Bathing in its healing waters, all is well.
  Thus, my love is as the well that doth water;
  And when the sickness is cured, all is well.

Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Lest guilt's dark veil conceal thy light.
As, for instance, when we first met,
Your beauty graced my heart, thus:
"Had I known then, 't meant no injury,
To blind me with false charms."
So then, love, forgive my hasty trespass,
And know it's but right that you've known
To love with a steadfast heart, to stay
As daylight may appear before a storm.
  Or call it hope, that springs from depths of glove,
  And shines for love, and for me, a beacon.

In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn,
And new falsehoods of thy soft saucy youth spread.
For swerving hither to sodomize me,
Thou art the true traitor, in my knowledge,
And, bitter mistress, in my love dislikes;
Possessing thy sweet power to love,
A mistress' treachery, in love's sweet spite.
  Leaving thy beauty to me, thou hast power to know
  The boundaries of love, with me defined.

Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep;
Dian saw her chance, and slept through it,
A gift from her lover's hand she did not quell.
But when Love's ghost adorned his brow,
A beauty's tear fell on his sleep-filled eyelids,
A lesson drawn from Love's own stolen art.
So, to prevent such art from marring eyes,
I'll confess, I often dream of you,
My love's sweet portrait, in dreams I see you everywhere.
  In truth, I often do see you,
  In dreams, your shadow casts its light.

The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
Came dancing by; one maiden fair, so fair,
Stained with her finger when his fire burned,
A death-bed confession of his passion she too,
Came dancing too, to share this flame,
And to him she said, "since from you I learn,"
"This love, which taught me to write,
To give life after death, keeps alive my love."
  This she said, and so proved false,
  For he, in sleep, burnt with a fervent brand.

When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Though she knows my value, and knows not my will,
So I believe her, though she knows it is a fallacy,
And in this she seems to me ignorant;
But why should I believe her false, she whose lie I so willingly?
Because she loves me, and I am deceived,
And because she loves me, and knows it,
I believe her false, and so am doomed to fall,
A prey to her whimsical despair.
  But why do I not depart from this despair?
  Because she loves me, and I am determined to fall.

Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And him I cheer up with my sorrows hard;
Unlooked for aid my joy I cry,
And with my blackest tears beseech the skies
To give my sovereign's peace a stormy night.
In this state of things I find my source,
Of gladness and of sighs, a knot;
But when that same source doth impart
A sorrow to my rose, I find yet more grief,
And more hate, in love's strange policy.
  For sweetest things hate those that are strong,
  And loathed by those that are dear.
