1
Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Enhancing the very plants that thy shame disfigures?
Why do I cherish not improved conditions,
That thou, thyself so fair and grand,
Bestow on wretched earth dignity art?
Give me respite from this melancholy,
That I may peruse thy fair face anew,
And find healing balm in thy essence.
  Seek'st thou to claim what thou shouldst depart?
  By self-example, die for thy sins, and live a golden sheen.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
My heart enthralled by the false, its sweet deceit.
The true cure, the balm of my soul's face,
Is this, that which doth heal the sick mind,
Making outward proof of the ties that bind,
And hides the sickness from its array.
  In love's deceit, the heart is sick,
  And in love's mockery, the doctor feigned.


3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
Spiteful cold and yearning for shadows?
Nay, my vision is quite clear,
Yet my judgment surely errrs in that regard;
Not seeing the faces that clock will chronicle,
Of thee, what constancy doth love impart,
Departing not a single blessed shadow's trace.
O poor executor of love's diadem,
Whose very looks are bought with borrowed tears,
Whose painted visage doth counterfeit love's gilded race,
Who receives this love and this love's evidence,
Who hath so much beauty stolen from thee?
 


4
Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot
My own nature, which proves most vain,
To work works with thy aid and increase thee?
Dost thou love some phantom that proves more than me
And shares my numbers with thy trifling skill?
Ay, if I could read in thy looks how
To blind thy heart with numbers happy it makes,
Thy creator would praise thy big name and say
"This doting heart doth shape the shape of a tomb."
  If some penitent shall find this written,
  I'll be knave as haddock undone.

O, from what power hast thou this powerful might

To make me give the lie to my true sight,
To let evil chance define
Before I tell the truth and confess thyself?
Indeed, 'tis not from this love that I pay
The hardest toll of all my foes;
From thy enmity, purest love's bane grow,
And my love swears by thy twisted view.
O, what vile venom doth love inject
In my heart, which should grow to such deadly lore
If ever it fell under thy loving rule.
  Yet, love, refrain from evil; evil will not take thee.
  Thy good name and thy eternal flame shall thrive.

Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Lest you become entangled in my wrongs.
Your virtue, so esteemed, can claim no prize
Without my full satisfaction;
So, use deceit, and I shall surely stop
At nothing to keep you near.
O, chide me not, for I have done
What I ought to do, yet did not fulfill it;
Ensnared by your passion, I have twisted
Truth's chain, and bound my soul to fall.
  Yet, love, maintain your disdain; I am losing

In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn.
How then mightst thou thy vow endure, if still alive
When my eyes once admitted ne'er a blind lover's hue?
How couldst love presage thy transgression so remote,
In thy youth wooing once married thy heart?
O, be silent, dear heart; so long as I dare
Thou art methought my soul's cherished mate;
Then view thy double falsehood with pleasure.
  But thy injustice may be shown more blameworthy still,
  Being most amiss being praised most.

Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep.
So, to cure the injury, she tied a brand on his cheek,
And beat the lover to death with a comb.
At last, he awaked and wailed,
For Love, he said, had taken his heart away.
The truth was, his heart was with his lover's eyes,
And Love, to have regained it, would have to die.
  I beguiled by cunning she played the fool,
  And found my love's true cost: she'd stitched him shut in her wardrobe.

When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Who no knowledge of beauty knows,
Despite the praise whereupon she excels.
Till Nature takes pity on me, though I rise,
And plants my blood in some bower,
Where verdant growths meet eroding head,
Her pride seems lifted, my grief diminished,
And I, thank heaven, am free from
The tyrant poison that takes hold of my heart.
  Still, I love her, and her fame withstands
  That she may keep my love's sweet name.

Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And his dove sweet love doth cherish me dear;
The lesser angel is an evergreen fair,
And oft times his dove do wake or sigh.
So when tempests obscure his fair face,
Or life tarnishes his gentle aspect so,
I do think of him, even then, and so speak,
For then my sweet Lord solace stands thus,
Two true loves with me in their right place.
  Thus the evening is sweet, when our love rest
  In love, where our shadows sway.
