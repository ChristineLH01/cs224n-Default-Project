Those lips that Love's own hand did make
Breathed forth the sound that said "I hate"
To me that languished for her sake;
Which, strange to say, had the might
To conquer the weakness of my breast,
And to stand against the steel of her vow,
The hardest I've known in mine own hand.

Of selfsame love, strangely enough
Changed their aspect with those lips that beseech
A warmer love to bear their pain;
Now looking kind, they say "I forgive"
And hold their change with gentle sighs;
Thirdly, they look kinder still, but hold
Their love with sighs that slowly grow;

Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
When every private canist finds his due?
Grant me the pardon: my love well knows her virtue,
Though she repents, I will not forsake thee.
To thee, I send this written edict,
To make known my love's virtue,
Though she repents, I will not quell.
She knows her duty, and so doth enforce
The penalty of my disobedience, heavy heart and sight.
  Hence, I'll pen this edict, so that at least
  My love may know her worth, even when I'm gone.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
Craving that which the heart cannot hitch.
The best way to kill a fever is to cure it,
And cure its fevers by gentle methods;
But that is not my case.
My love is soick, its walls are eternally
Unyielding, its hold so tight,
That when this sad state befalls thee,
Thy heart may break, yet lives again,
In thrall to that which gave thee life.
  Thus, I return, chastened but not defeated,
  And find that medicine hath no power to clear.


3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
Which sees yet the bare truth as if not so?
O, me, which do you approve as my greatest fault,
That I in my judgment not so true a man?
Because in my judgment your beauty lies,
My judgment is almost as blind as your face.
Mark how one man in his thousand can see
Another's beauty better than his own,
Since all men's eyes in this vast world are out.
  In one of their innumerable windows do I see
  The sun almost everywhere springing to light.


4
Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot
My own life, to leave poor widowed and bare?
Who hates thee that I name as my ally?
On whom dost thou frown that I do adore?
On whom dost thou frown that I do adore?
Who do I honor that I do despise?
On whom dost thou scowl that I do adore?
Indeed, if thou dost scowl on me, I have found
A reason to be very harsh to thee.
  My love is strengthened by thy fond-hearted esteem,
  And by that esteem I find many a just offense.


5
O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
And for a fee greater proof lie tell
Than if I could bear the blush of shame.
O, let me endure the pain, the shame,
The sight of which, in spite of thy hand,
Strikes fear in every bone, binds
My heart to follow where thou desires it to go.
Let me suffer, though torment be the name,
This torment, if it do bring me to thee,
  I'll bear it, though it be the cross of hell.
  For thou art too cruel, and I am too kind.


6
Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Lest sorrow embolden my silent sigh;
Let love's sweet power speak louder than my dull complaint.
If you're caught, confess with me,
My love is false, yet I love you twice as dearly,
Sometime more than once, on different days;
In each love, I find my reasons,
And in your love, my reasons are dressed.
Nurture compassion in that breast, so blessed,
That compassion may merit sympathy indeed.
  And thus, lovely youth, you'll bear fruit,
  When grief bends to mercy, and love bends to teach.


7
In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn,
As marriages contracted before thine oath were sworn.
Were it not sinful for thee to forsake me,
After my most solemn vows' account,
As shooting death on the spur of the moment,
Thou art as impious a heart
As that which leads a miser to spend;
For thriftless theft such strings are tied
As make the most delicate jewel seem brittle and lame.
  For thee I'll tell thee how:--
  Thou hast love instilled in thee, and born that way.


8
Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep.
A woman's gentle art could not compare,
Nor his fierce passion could the fierce fire tame.
So she plunged into a well nearby,
And there, within the crystal sea, he fell asleep.
The waters kept his essence, though she purified it,
A gift from Love's own artistry she gave.
She returned with a cure, this time a triumph,
A potion that eased his discomfort, but left him distraught.
  I fear this woman's art may have borrowed from his grace,
  But I fear no worse for a love-kindled hearth.


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
Sought after their own budding love, with much sacrifice;
And few maiden fairs did away
With their vows till this fiery brand
Expelled the sorrow from hearts everywhere.
This brand, which burned with such fire,
Mastered innocence, making alive
The dull flesh of the maiden lovely,
Who for love of you, through no fault of hers,
Had boundless beauty made her lame,
Crowning thee, she set her law;
  But, out of pure evil, she did this:
  Stats of thy deeds, well knowing, she burned with lust.


10
When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Speak well of you, yet not be so kind;
Though she may be mistaken, I believe her
Of true things, and so should you,
Unless you for my sake were painted false,
And added to her praise did take part.
This I do vow, and this shall I hold,
Unless my love be marred by your crime;
  In that case, I will not swear, though I may not say,
  For I must be mistaken, and so should she.


11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And he with me is as deluded as I;
For he hath sworn by heaven's pure grace,
By the faithlessness of my sway.
Yet, nor can his love for me be so swerving,
As my inferior's affections tend to sway.
He quotes the star to heaven, his reason lends,
And says in heaven's name my soul doth lie;
But, like him, I am a sinner, and must live.
  For I have sworn by thy eternal grace,
  And by thy loving-kindness doth attest.
