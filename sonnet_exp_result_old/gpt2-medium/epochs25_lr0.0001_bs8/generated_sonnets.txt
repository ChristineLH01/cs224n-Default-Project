Those lips that Love's own hand did make
Breathed forth the sound that said "I hate"
To me that languished for her sake;
Whose touch, as the waves make towards the pebbled shore,
Creep in upon the wood and break against the wave.
M. de Chambly, in his passion still,
Sung this hymn to me: "O, lover, where did you filch your charm
If not from my beloved's lips, which with passion bake?
Take heed, lest in singing this verse,
Your sweet voice loses her gentle charm!"
  Lascivious lips, to whom Love so willingly took
  Their filching tool, fell to smiting each other with blows.


1
Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Raising thy dead weight upon dead brass?
Why bestow such riches on this fading edifice,
A monument destined to turn to dust?
Shall the inheritors of this vice,
Consume thy legacy, thy very essence?
Then, soul, feed on the anguish of thy servant,
Let them fade, while you flourish and transcend.
Exchange the transient hours for eternal bliss,
Find solace within, while lacking without.
  So shall you feast on Death, who devours all souls,
  And when Death is vanquished, no more shall we know death's toll.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
And spite of which my soul acheth today.
The cure is to be found, the remedy sweet,
In the bosom of my soul, where its sweet scent doth dwell.
The outward guise of love is best,
In my breast, where its sweet scent doth live;
But what truly well is love, in thee,
Its true worth unseen, in deeds so vile,
Hidden from the eyes of men, ever to be.
  In this vice, love finds sweet delight,
  And medicine to soothe the soul.


3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
That for false eyes all good is willfully straying?
O, no, let me see what my eyes can say,
That is, to heaven's add, of you, of you they have no part.
Be contented, my dear, with that which is plain to see,
If not converted, at least with a plea of better,
By your being, as my mind is, made better.
  Seek'st thou to claim what is thine, which is not thy?
  By self-example, thus thou mayst be proved untrue.

5
O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
And in thy falsehood do methinks I live!
That tongue that tells the story of thy days,
Making me out to be untrue,
Thou gav'st me truth itself to thy dishonesty.
Make me answer that thou wilt be true
And I will be false to thee;
That is, to thy shame, I answer,
And so shall we drink the cup
Of bitter love, for we both must be false.
  Thus shall we drink the double strain: bitterness and care.
  Our bitter love is led to seek the sweetest of all potions.


6
Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Lest you become entwined in my transgressions.
For, as you lead me astray, I forsake
My nobler self to my body's treachery.
My soul whispers to my flesh that it may soar
In love; the flesh, no longer a bore,
Rises at your name, its power to explore,
A triumphant trophy, yours to adore.
  And thus, in pride, it serves, always near,
  To rise and fall, for your love, my dear.


7
In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn,
And new love sworn in old offenses sworn.
How can I cherish thee but by thy assenting,
And for that treasure where is my deserving?
O, give me leave to doubt, paranoid heart,
That thy sweet love may stay an enemy,
Though I am as unjust as thou know'st.
For how do I trust thy pure heart, so strange,
When I am sure of wrong, suspecting thee?
  To trust thou me not to love wrong,
  To trust myself, even though I do wrong.


8
Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep;
The flame leapt from his touch, a celestial sight,
Exceeded even the might of the unthrifty night.
The waters kept the fire's essence, though, alive,
A bubbling, healing source for all ailments dear;
But when this source ran dry, Love's true shame came to light,
A curse laid upon him, a heavy heart,
And ruined his very soul.
  For Love's fire warms the heart, soaks the mind,
  And when this source is lost, no healing breast.


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
Pronounced his name as such, to shame his captors.
The maiden who for love of beauty did vow,
But scorn of beauty, for true love's sake fell away,
And left her beauty dishonored, and lame;
Thus I, made lame, learn to sing
His sacred hymns, which pierced through both air and wall,
Making love anew, through new air,
A melody by hearts and lungs that belong.
  Thus, I'll live, through your love, my poor lungs;
  And other poor lascivious deaths, which should not be.


10
When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Though not to love, yet, to thee, I did lend
My heart to believe in her falsity;
But, lo, she loves not only me,
But I, and every man beside,
Do shamelessly swerve to their own part,
And nothing proves their love but by my name.
  In some strange sense I love her,
  For I no other think I know her falsehood.


11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And in his might I find my solace;
The other my muse doth write her rude song,
Which shall above that bitterest sound be.
I must not mention the sweet pleasure
Of either, though in mine eyes a thousand sweets stand,
For that would spoil my love even more.
My worse angel is fair like a lamb,
Lean and quick as a hunted bear;
