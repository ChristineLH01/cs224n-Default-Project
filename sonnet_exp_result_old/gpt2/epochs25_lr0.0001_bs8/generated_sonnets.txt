Those lips that Love's own hand did make
Breathed forth the sound that said "I hate"
To me that languished for her sake;
Her cheek, touched by compassion's fire,
Taught her tongue to sing a sweeter choir,
And "I" reduced to tears, as had she gone.
"I" for love, moved by her,
Sanctioned the pain with her eulogy,
Creating a new sort of heaven
That Love's own fair might add,
Replacing sorrow with sweetness,
Creating art that revived what was once lost.
  "I" by love returned fire to find,
  And "I" by compassion, by accident,
  Both for love's sake and for gain.

Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Denying the very thing that thou deserv'st?
Why should I seek to usurp thy boon,
Overrule all other sources of charm,
Thy foe, thy true heart, thy sensual plea?
In me, thou hast no ally, no soft confidant,
But as the stars, in thy constancy bound,
Your outward shines with such brilliance,
Though in my breast, thou dost indirectly wend.
  For thou hast no love, no faith,
  And in me, no faith at all.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
Alternating 'tis vouchsafe from thine eye,
For fear of which 'tis from thine own soul,
Myself at war with myself against.
Possessing strength, I fear,
As a willing patient doth return
With bloody show to absolve me of injury,
Yet 'tis my love that 'twixt fevers,
For itting grace, I fear, 'twixt pain and triumph.
  Pitying me, then, is my duty,
  To watch over the love that 'twixt heart and death.


3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
That alone alters love's great design?
If deemed fair be the view most gracious,
What worth the world, that deems it otherwise?
If not so fair, then love's subtle charm
Is but sweet ignorance, obscured by all men's reproachful cries.
How can love's beauty be so writ,
Or on my spirit's cranium anchored,
That it may outlive first the eyes?
  Love's fire warms the desert, and so should all men's eyes,
  But eyes, love's fire warms the swarms of men.


4
Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot
My own identity by thee bound?
Who hates thee that I name as my ally?
Then should I question if I am false in all
Enumerate what I do within myself.
Mark how once I have removed myself
From all that I adore, and scold thee all alone,
Do I not then praise thee more,
When I by thee am truly blest?
  Thy allure shows what a friend thou dost thine eyes
  To adore me even more.


5
O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
To sway him from his course, and show him how far
He belongs to my heavy learning.
O, know, sweet deception! Now believe me, of thy heart!
My love knows, and will obey,
The surest thing from thee, so kindly shown;
So thou cunning slanderer! How can I keep thee so poor,
When thou thy love is as strong as heaven's own?
  Take heed, sweet deception! Thy love's most precious treasure,
  Shall be my waste, if thou dost pilfer it.


6
Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Lest you become entangled in my wrongs.
For, as you deceive me, I betray
My nobler self to my body's treachery.
My soul whispers to my flesh that it may soar
In love; the flesh, no longer a bore,
Rises at your name, its power to explore,
A triumphant trophy, yours to adore.
  And thus, in pride, it serves, ever so near,
  To rise and fall, for your love, my dear.


7
In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn,
As youth hath no faith in his rite,
So basest hearts think thee to be twain
And wink a gracious reproach to thy loveliest name.
For how can love's sweet name endure
When thy transgressions prove thy honor allayed?
Nor canst thou then claim my good name endure
When thine enemies, thy enemies seem powerless.
  As on thy bed, thou double shame'st
  And womanhood bears witness to this trait.

Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steepen.
A cure for lovers' pains, the waters did take,
And when I was gone, my love's sweet supplication
Replaced that fierce poison pent in my breast.
But not for the lovers' eyes, which I had journeyed 'round the world,
Nor for their lips, which the waters had craved,
Nor his own heart, which so frequently err;
  Neither for nor against, his love nor mine own plight,
  Died for love, and that ransom I kept.


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
Died in their fiery baths, alone.
Then asked of his soul, who reigned over hearts,
If it could impart wisdom, birth a king,
To save the world from despair and from grief?
He repented, quaffed his drink and straightway returned,
Sated by night for many a nymph's grace,
Yet from henceforward no more his love-story would take place.
  For love was never broken in his love;
  Still, as he left his heart, it might seem death's reign took its leave.

Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
Though not so fair as thy fair should make.
For thou art fair as any child of mine,
Though not so bright as mine eye can make thee glow.
And yet my loving heart, dimmed by love's tear,
Shines downcast on my unworthy dream.
My love, thus, I cast away with tears,
The one fair, shining star that gives me peace,
The other kindling despair and mock sorrow.
I love thee, and I cannot argue
Against thy fair, loving heart, thy scorn.
