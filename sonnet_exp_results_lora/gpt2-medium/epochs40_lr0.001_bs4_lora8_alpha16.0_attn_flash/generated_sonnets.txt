Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Seeing thy wealthy moiety crammed
Full of consumers, filling the world with strangers?
Didst thou themselves become ill
To hinder thy freedom, preferring another?
Thou wast thyself the kidnap and ransom
Of innocent Tritons by their crippling addiction,
Were poverty thee alone, poor one,
While all the world built in on thee.
O, be that selfish thought, that somewhat surprising
Reward of myself that thine equates so,
Save that by myself thy worth wanes.
  Remember, dear soul, that thou art stronger
  Than that glutton by whom he consumes.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
A fever that cures not well, though there be a disease.
If, O patient disease, signs are yeedy,
For a day hath this been more strongly shaken,
Thy flower hath died and thy leaves must come;
If these pleas are not answered, thy pain is great.
The sick man is as a beast doth grow,
Yet is not crazy, nor goes on sick,
Although he continues never thir' hour, to drive thee away.
  For man, being a little sick, grows wild,
  that t' patient with wild fever doth chase.


3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
Without an element in it of earth to give life?
Or if they have no eyes to give speech,
So hath love itself speech, and truth no doubt;
The parts that make it are made without me,
And to me in an outsider their worth lies.
So I have eyes in heaven yet doubting myself,
Whereto earth has no breath to quench his rosy cheeks.
  But let them be judged with the many stars in heaven,
  Where beauty and truth live, and all balance in accord.

5
O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
I must verify thy love by symbols of love,
And yours by thine own truth.
Thou, who wilt continue virtue in an insubstantial state,
Accursed tongue that by unwitting rude esteem
Cannot lend any sound to my rhyme.
Nay, why, better say I, I love thee this more:
If I had more tongue for better understanding,
Thou arts sufficient I can lay down a base.
  Then do not grudge me that abundance I have;
  My reward will be good thanks only to thee.


6
Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
That by yielding is not christened love;
For thou as a mother's child
Thou art first wife's love, dear child of fair intent,
And all thine own conscience confesseth
That thou lov'st too soon.
Thou most devoted lov'st not till the ere too late,
Or else disloyal ministers are employed
To consecrate to praise beauty's thought twain.
  Since pleasing too soon attracts the dull,
  Be mindful of thy statefulness and avoid being deceived.

8
Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep
The foulest flowers to the ground.
The cause was passion; the result hatred;
Hate burned by hatred, fume doth cause;
And red poison in flames catches sweet fruits where
It doth sting the ground.
"Shall not my fire burn in hatred and hate in breath
If my maid lay down his fire to love's defense?"
"Nor thy sweet maid should kill him for thy love's crime,"
"But thou safety's lure proves thy crime."
  My fire then was a flame, but it no flame doth burn;
  Its poison lies dead and dead


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep
Lifted eyelids from his dying gaze,
Resumed murmuring to look on the fresh moon;
Before long all days were black and ugly night,
And for the play-divined rose
Were swallowed up with graves made sweet by bloom;
At last beauty with thy semblance had met
Within some thickening ripened burrow deep in deep night;
And stood out to lonely day on yonder bank
Which even under the obscuring moon Hath reserved for thee.
  Yet death no longer curses him with horror;
  He looks on thy beauty, and keeps on smiling till all are gone.


10
When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Who, through her ignorance of my kind,
Who no vouchsafe to my pale dear child,
Cannot peaceably decline words that thy gentle breast
Suitable for beautiful thought say.
So I do love her by many hidden precludes,
Since, as my reverent priest confesseth,
She is of my love a feigned lie,
Whilst I was assured her of that truth she knew.
  Thus if she so deceiveth me, what price hath my love set?
  She deceives me neither so much as her gracious grant gives,
  For to her was my praise


11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And for my sins and those of his friend
A gentle, refined, well-disciplined
Treasured holy creature.
That is true though I commonly of false wits say,
And timorous fables so invented;
My loving sovereign though too true to me
To be manipulated by stratagems gazillioned,
Still keeps me in his degree.
No man may deny him though, nor in his rigging
Thy statutes and his excellent bosom prove;
Him me therfore I will keep men in high mind
By proofs planted on thy breast till it be expired.
