Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
When every tid, every word, offers thee relief?
Is it thy will, thy self-loving heart,
To suffer the contrite closure of thy name,
For that thou art so grounded am not well?
O, no. Thy love, though much, is not so great.
It is my love that keeps mine eye awake,
Mine own true love that doth my rest defeat
To play the watchman ever for thy sake.
  For thee watch I whilst thou dost wake elsewhere,
  From me far off, with others all too near.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
That too much moisture doth lend to the disease.
I love not sick people, though I believe in sickness
To lose my friend, my beloved physician;
I have not heard the swell of that which doth cover his head.
If there be nothing new, but that which is
Hath been before, how are his pupils beguiled?
I grant, sweet love, your beauty is a spring;
And yet, being fever, I cannot cure.
  Presume not on thy summer, for I love not summer
  Unless thou get some fresh blood from my nurse.

O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
Which in their wills of love worked
To make my imperfect shade true?
O, none, unless this shadow doth really inhabit
In my seeing, that I in my judgment may see

And, as the sun is daily new and old,
Doth him grace his day with golden oil,
Which scarce now but in his power grow.
  And yet methinks I have eyes quite out of my understanding,
  And they, though my soul's influence grow,
  As true physicians do when they look into thy part.

7
In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn.
For when thyself returning home
Thou dost leave me half wroth, half pained,
My life hath in thought and feeling grown,
And I, a wretch, find a new friend.
Thou art as vile a judge
As any mother, though not so bright
As those gold candles fixed in heaven's eye.
  If my faint hope that thou in me dost lie,
  Lack I gon deem thee to be slain.

Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steepen
In her brow, where beauty's priceless skill
Lifts up the afflicted's brow; and, sore from fatigue,
Sap checked with frost, she looked on thee
And fell asleep.
Then, straight seeking a new moon,
The pride of his spring cried:
"Thou best of dearest and mine only friend
Art left the world, the world in love with thee."
  But thence this passion grew, and, becoming restless,
  As if by some instinct the spring were loved,
  As if by some odor the bud grew cold.


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
At the sight of his sleeping face,
O'er-snowed and pale, dreading the winter's near.
At length some apparition do appear
Which sleepers never saw,
Who, all alone, pour their loving praise
On him that doth cover every blot.
 'Tis thee, love-god, that keeps watch
For every shames and every insult committed,
And keeps his lamp in the living flame;
  Whate'er thy thoughts or thy heart's workings be,
  For nimble love or malicious hate doth play such part.

When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
When I was wont to greet her with my lays,
As she hath herself impregnable,
By shining in courage by adversity;
And for a tyrant like me she hath power,
Although I conceive she hath no joy left.
In nothing art thou art wooed but by thy sources,
And thence my love confounds, becoming a cripple,
Sap checked with a tomb.
  Lascivious grace, in whom all ill well shows,
  Kill me with spites, yet we must not be foes.


11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
Whose face with mine own weakness is familiar;
And in that I admire him greatly.
However, love is not so gentle
As in his tones, though his black is more bright.
In him my love shines more bright
Than in his piping, which methinks is still hammered.
And for a man so beloved do I hold,
As Christ's elect, yet not so eager
To make his object desired again.
  Therefore my love is so rooted,
  That I cannot blame him for my sake.
