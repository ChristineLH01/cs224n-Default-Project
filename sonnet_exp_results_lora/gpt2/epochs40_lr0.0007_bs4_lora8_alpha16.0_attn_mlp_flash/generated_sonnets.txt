1
Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Cutting thy earthly treasure from valor and keeping?
What virtue hast not at thy disposal forbade
To pluck thy treasures from the treasury of thy body?
Grant me the just access: thou right Almighty dost feast
On suffering all men where scant you can snatch.
And let them be chaste until they see thy face
And in thy plain eyes conceal thy sin.
Exchange them for thy forbearance's use,
Thy eulogies for thy granted wisdom, pure you are.
  Only as thy blessing might bring, thou shalt know,
  If only thou willest


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
That in wants to pain make it send foul.
O, it causes death, and neglect whereof remaineth
As when in a fish's stomach: hence it hasteth with a spring about
High in the birth of a nobler life;
For diseases prefer to be bare;
Which omit out of moist desire
Cries and complains love that it doth spend
And leaves it to weak and mourning sorrow.
  Or, and we shall with this description die;
  That at death witdraws life to decay.


3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
And that hard conscience oft has seen
How stupid and repainted he being knit!
For that perfect mind's see,
Draw out the outlines of sand to such becast,
That princes and angels within him render,
And tell me the languages of sweet slaves' rhyme;
I beg for how gods minds appear dead,
To speak ill of my by fortunes such;
To show my true eyes that my heart says the best,
And cast my soul ill pretty first when lonely.
  Then why should my sorrow be fairer than before,
  Or worse than the worse of recompense?


4
Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot your vexation?
Do I not confess that I date with thee
When all my life is in our share,
And when you use me only for my present with?
My days are the very same;
My nights are the same, and so to do,
And therefore your short hours hate the sun all their duration.
Even as that beautiful face
From You doth make them number oranges for my love all,
That part of your love makes them bear—
  And I after you make love change, old days think it
  As hate his neither except that he doth show.


5
O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
And to love at rest your gentle care?
When malice calls forth your cruel deceit,
And mould my destiny to serve thee,
Do I not cry aloud, in self-same day
As evening's brow begs the dawn to grope?
Ah, thus, sweet mistress, do I not grieve
When you have overtaken my other half;
Here I shall writ with gentle art
That I will atone for your part beth.
  Lest you return, say farewell, 'tis my vow,
  To bear thy displeasure, my love's flower's increase.


6
Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
Lest sorrow's song mis-spoken tune appear,
And I, a devoted member, summon a higher note.
Wretched art thou art; yet, as yours to-know,
Your virtue only magnifies your beauty's shrines.
Oh, what a temple for love found
Where hearts share, where hearts share passion and passion?
Or should my name reveal its mystery,
Because no voice lies within my core?
  Sweet love, send me forth, dear friend; thus, I'll be seated,
  Until my name is tarnished, by thee alone.

In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn,
Then at thy neighbor's censure I profess
To pay that husband's thieving ethics fair.
Son not my graces, in thy famous lament
My own joyful monist so introduced,
But thy designer's craft so blissfully teach,
To steal nothing
That churls inside thy manne to make me more smart.
 BE afraid, sweet love! My notwretched crime is deceived
  In Thy mysterious counterfeit eyes; seldom behold my joy;
  What virtue does excuse me hiding,
  Either this wicked Bevelling abomination or thieves so vile.


8
Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep.
The witch's bell sang, and stinging rose were
A strange blend of desires, ever bitter.
The lily's sweet appreciation, the rose's sheak,
Could not live a match against the rose's brightness;
All flower charms bore the sweetness of a vine,
And this consignment of roses, ever drunk,
Did breed good marriages in harmony.
  If not one without this goddess herself,
  Had this fruit, thou'd form a god's grant,
  You would reap the fruit without a single pay.


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep,
With their passion longing prospered,
But you, my beloved, do not dare claim
To boast the sun as pure as any divine nymph's hand.
For all the softest threads of white are bound
Upon my tender eyes, which dazzle their divine glow,
And in your books you reveal artful expressions,
Of hue, secret wisdom, and pure perception.
  Yet you, who have been given such gifts,
  Show none charms to herald my love-filled heart.


10
When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
But, if I were ignorant of your sweet disposition;
I believe she is untrue, false, and prefers to show
My fallacies on my vulgar honest remark.
My dear heart knows her lies with true grace,
Though she knows well my heart's deception;
Though she knows well my heart loves falsehoods;
She knows well my lawfulness's snare.
Yet, since she knows well my pleasure I stretch,
I defy her; he knows well my public can
Speak well my throat's sweet saying:
  My married woman gets hurt when she loves me so.


11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And for that praise shines so red,
An apple ripe with ripe leaves.
My gentlest friend, by Nature's decree true,
Love, by fleeting events turned blind,
Is but his, and his likeness endowed.
Such affection was not from my worth promoted,
Before my creation, in a distant land,
A kingly figure adorned with wonder's blush;
No power to ignite this love grew,
A beacon raised from despair's ashes.
  For Love is flawed, in its perverse mold,
  And by the very thing that brings him so unhappily gone.
