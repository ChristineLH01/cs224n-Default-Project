Those lips that Love's own hand did make
Breathed forth the sound that said "I hate"
To me that languished for her sake;
I thought that a heart so happy
Could love my bosom so well,
And as I thought so she gave the same answer.
And what do I tell that sweet face
By whose sweet tongue I shall be in love
In spite of her love?
My love, I love, is not equal to her love,
And, seeing she shows it, I hate it.


1
Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
The glory of a happy marriage;
Whose gladness thou dost not boast?
You say I love thee, and dost love it well,
That I do my part to the good of all;
But I love you to love me most.
  You do me your love's worth,
   And I, in your love's worth, do your love's worth.


2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
In what neglect doth my love doth repair;
And since my love's eyes are even as cold as their breath,
And my love's nostrils are as a man's,
I fear I should be unfaithful to thee.
  Yet I'll love no man but my love,
  Which will not deny aught that thou dost show
  To others, nor in thyself dost slander me.



3
O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
That not a single eye doth give me thanks,
Which doth feel my sweet love's boundless love's sway?
I do not look upon my love, nor do I abide
By that which it doth say, but what is not thought of.
  I love not what it doth say, and I love not what it doth say.
  And I love the truth, that I love both;
  If love be so, then love is not of me,
  But what it doth say is my love.


4
Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot
Thy pity and thy sweet name?
Or do I not remember thy grace,
Which I bear to thee from heaven?
Therefore thou shalt say, I love thee not.
But though I love thee,
I love not myself in thy love,
Nor do I esteem thee worthier of praise,
Nor do I say this is of my love
Than that thou dost think so,
Nor do I say this is of mine.
O, my love, love me not, but speak truth
To the world, so that others may know
How good it is to love with truth.


5
O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
To take away the true joy from the fair world,
To make me treat my love as if it had none?
In what way have you this power, this power?
I fear it to be a false speech, a mortal thing.
What power can this art give thee, if thou art not
My friend, my mistress, my daughter?
What power can this art give thee, if thou art not thyself?
O, I love thee to see me more fair than thou art.
If thou art not thyself, then will I be thy lover.
  The fair world is thy mistress


6
Love is too young to know what conscience is;
Yet who knows not conscience is born of love?
Then, gentle cheater, urge not my amiss,
I think what you have said is true, yet, after
Loved is what is false, I lie.
  Therefore, thou mind'st, if love be born of love,
  Then I know love's meaning, but love's meaning is found in love,
  And no sorrow is yet mine.


7
In loving thee thou know'st I am forsworn,
But thou art twice forsworn, to me love swearing;
In act thy bed-vow broke, and new faith torn.
Thou art my soul, to whose love thou shalt not wish,
When, having done thee thee pleasure, thou dost keep;
But when, having done thee no good, thou dost do me no wrong,
Thy love thyself is, thou know'st it not,
But thou art both my love and mine.
  I love thee, and love thee not, that is true love.


8
Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep,
And his lamp did sing, sweet as ivory;
But his night-maker in his fury was;
His heart too full of fear, and his tongue too bold,
For his wife's right hand hath been taken.
All the gods have decreed his death,
And the purest maiden is no more.
  All these they say, but thy mother's fair,
  As the night-light shines from her sweetbed.


9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep
Have borne his eye, and brooded upon his sight.
This sweet appetite, that feeds so sweetly on love,
Shall have no less terror on the young than upon the old,
Whilst all the wise men, and all the wise men's children,
Were fair to be their watch-men, and their faces their eyes.
  But the serpent's cunning is most potent
  That, when, by night, he devours the young,
  He turns to sleep, and the young to sleep,
  And bears his prey to the old.


10
When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
With nothing but a thimble of truth in my sight.
'Twas a fellow whom I loved so dear that I loved his name
For the love of his name, but since he hath lost it,
'Tis all I have left of him, save his love to me.
  'Tis my love, and he's my treasure,
  That love's beauty hath but a shade of truth.


11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
And when he sees me but trouble and ill,
His love doth trouble him to find out,
And in his love I keep so many things that
His grief makes it hard to bear,
But so many are in me that my love gives me
The pleasure of his love,
And so they to me do give their love.
  This shall be my care till I die;
  All that I can leave will be left with you,
  And then I shall leave you as a dearer thing.
